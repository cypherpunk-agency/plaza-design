# ACCESS DENIED: story and learning progression

Working concept, consolidated with Tommi on 2026-10-16. This is a design proposal, not a
description of the running game or an implementation instruction. Everything remains revisable.

## 1. Direction and status

Player testing reports that the opening introduces the world and mechanics too quickly,
too early, and with too much competing information. Extending dialogue alone will not solve
this: the game needs a deliberate order of experiences, practice, capabilities and places.

**Agreed direction:** keep the world glide; give ordinary life a substantial playable
prologue; begin at the harbor around sundown, work a guided night shift, travel home to
Downtown, then experience identity deletion in the morning. Act I belongs primarily to
Downtown. Hacking is learned gradually as a way to recover agency. Introduce further
concepts through later acts rather than teaching the whole game at the start.

**Working story proposals:** a ripperdoc enables hacking through augmentation; an incident
later forces a rescue into a robot body; Act II includes trying to recover the human body;
another transition removes the robot body and introduces nonphysical action in Act III.
The cause, responsibility, timing and permanence of these events are open. A possible
ripperdoc abduction and an excursion outside Downtown for the Act I finale are also open.

**Recommended interpretation of deletion:** the Registry withdraws administrative recognition.
The player remains physically human, and acquaintances can still remember them. Official
services refuse access; people may help, exploit or avoid the player. Memory erasure,
invisibility and a literal spectral body do not automatically follow. “Ghost” initially
describes social and administrative exclusion. This interpretation gives the losses in later
acts distinct meanings; the precise technology still needs a fiction pass.

## 2. The story spine

| Phase | Player's situation and immediate desire | Scope | Learning responsibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prologue: the night shift | Finish work and get home; experience a life that functions, however unfairly | Guided harbor work area, commute, a small Downtown route, home | Movement, interaction, ordinary items, observation, routines |
| Act I: erased | Regain everyday agency, find help, understand why access vanished | Downtown in increasing depth; possible final excursion | Direct hacking, state, events, wires, clues and basic access |
| Act II: another body | Survive in a robot body and pursue recovery of the human body | Selected districts open progressively | Physical capabilities, logic, time, resources, security and systems in combination |
| Act III: beyond the body | Continue acting after losing the robot body; decide what restoration should mean | Networks connecting places and people already understood | Remote presence, distributed action, cooperation and consequential choices |

The progression is belonging → exclusion → another embodiment → action beyond embodiment.
Every loss must also change what the player can do. Preserve learned skills, relationships
and meaningful progress across transitions; avoid three repetitions of starting powerless.

The larger Registry conflict can remain: who is allowed to exist, who benefits from deletion,
and whether the player restores only themselves or changes the system for others. Recovering
the body gives Act II a personal objective. Existing fragments and faction errands should
support that objective with evidence, access or assistance rather than become a disconnected
collection task. The exact seven-fragment structure and old ending choices remain candidates.

## 3. Prologue: work, travel, home

The glide offers an ambient impression of the whole city, then settles at the harbor at dusk.
It should not demand that players learn district names or factions. Its existing sunrise,
route and train handoff need redesign to support this arrival; retaining the idea does not
commit us to its current duration or unskippable presentation.

The player works a night shift in harbor transport/logistics. Their exact job title is open;
the existing courier identity can fit this. The boss is a diegetic tutorial guide: short,
specific instructions attached to visible tasks. His demands reveal hierarchy, productivity
pressure and bureaucratic indifference. Narrative impatience must not create real deadlines,
repeated nagging or punishment for a new player learning the controls.

### Proposed sequence

| Beat | Player activity | New learning | Story and attention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P0: arrival | Take control and walk to the supervisor | Movement, destination cues | Shift begins; one workplace rather than a city to navigate |
| P1: assignment | Speak to the boss and approach the assigned object | Interaction and reading a short instruction | Authority, tone, the player's place in the workplace |
| P2: first delivery | Pick up and deliver an ordinary work item | Carrying and contextual use | Work has visible purpose; recognition makes access routine |
| P3: repeat with variation | Complete a second task with less instruction | Independent practice | Another worker becomes a person worth remembering |
| P4: small disruption | Resolve a simple physical obstruction or use a normal machine control | Observation, cause/effect, persistent state | Machinery follows rules; the boss prioritises throughput |
| P5: finish the shift | Return equipment, check out, receive or inspect pay | Familiar actions combined; exchange in context | Identity ties work, wages and movement together |
| P6: travel home | Board the expected service and get off Downtown | Guided travel and recognising a destination | Windows, passengers and notices broaden the world gently |
| P7: neighbourhood | Follow a short familiar route, encounter one recurring local | Practice interaction and orientation | Establish a relationship and one service that recognises you |
| P8: home | Use a few domestic objects, encounter Pixel, rest | Optional exploration and ending a routine | Establish ownership, comfort and something to lose |
| P9: morning rupture | Try a familiar action; seek an explanation | Recognising changed circumstances | A previously reliable service now refuses you; Act I begins |

These are beats to author, not a requirement for nine separate scenes. Harbor jobs must not
be disguised hacking exercises. Carrying a parcel and operating authorised controls are
enough. Security machinery, robots, wiring and schedules can be visible without becoming
systems the player must understand.

Time advances through completed activities and transitions: dusk → night → dawn commute →
home/rest → morning. Exact hours and the length of the rest remain open. The existing
05:41/06:04/09:40 sequence can inform staging but does not constrain it. A whole shift is a
fictional span, not a requirement to wait through a simulated shift or repeat filler jobs.
Allow breathing room and optional observations; advance required exposition at the player's pace.

## 4. Act I: Downtown becomes understandable

Begin with the loss of a familiar service, not a screen full of failures and new vocabulary.
Give the player time to attempt an ordinary response and speak to someone who recognises them.
The contradiction should be legible: “I know you” alongside “identity not found.”

The local arc moves from immediate needs to investigation:

1. Understand the denial and find human help.
2. Meet a ripperdoc through a local relationship, plausibly the fixer or Moth.
3. Acquire a limited first hacking capability and use it safely on one device.
4. Solve a second direct-action problem independently.
5. Learn one event-to-action wire, then repeat the concept in another context.
6. Recover access to a familiar service or home; feel a concrete gain in agency.
7. Follow evidence about Moth and the deletion through Downtown's interiors and rooftops.
8. Complete a local combination challenge. A motivated departure can lead to the finale.

**Clinic proposal:** reuse Dr. Kessel, already present in STORY.md, rather than inventing a
duplicate role. Place his first accessible treatment space in Downtown, or establish a local
visit/branch; his current Neon Market/Act II placement conflicts with this sequence. His
relationship to Moth is useful continuity. His earlier proposed data-selling motive is not
automatically the explanation for the incident.

**Resolve the home/clinic dependency:** the current game requires hacking to leave Capsule 7B.
If augmentation comes from a clinic, the player must be able to reach it first. Recommended
rewrite: ordinary egress remains possible, but re-entry or a household service is denied.
An assisted exit is another option. Do not require an ability before the story grants it.

Moth remains a friend and investigative thread; the fixer provides local help; Kessel provides
augmentation. Give each a clear role instead of having all three deliver overlapping tutorials.
Moth's message may point toward help or evidence, but no longer needs to unlock every interface.

Downtown depth can come from home, the stall, shop, library, yard, clinic, Moth's place,
service interiors and roofs. Opening a door can matter as much as opening a district.
Act I should not retain every current puzzle merely because it has been built.

## 5. Transitions and later acts: proposals to develop

### Act I to II

A possible treatment, upgrade or intervention goes wrong. Someone rescues the player into a
robot body, and Kessel may be taken away. This creates two linked leads: locate the body and
learn what happened to the person who could restore it.

The incident should follow a meaningful Act I success and grow out of established pressures.
Avoid making it an arbitrary punishment for trusting the game's required tutorial guide.
If the finale leaves Downtown, a return to the harbor offers a strong contrast with the
prologue: the player recognises a workplace but now encounters it without its protections.
How that encounter relates to the clinic incident and Moth's container is not yet decided.

### Act II

Begin with a short, safe reacclimatisation to the robot body. Familiar controls and hacking
knowledge carry over. Introduce one physical difference at a time. Possible capabilities
include different reach, carrying strength or machine access; these are options, not an
approved ability list. Do not add battery management simply because the body is mechanical.

Districts open in response to leads and capabilities, with bounded choices between routes.
This revises the older proposal that every fragment is available in any order immediately.
Logic, schedules, resource production and active security need their own practice spaces.
The body-recovery quest must make progress even if full restoration is delayed.

### Act II to III and Act III

The robot body is lost, destroyed, seized or becomes inaccessible; the exact event is open.
Remote presence must be established beforehand through small demonstrations or limited uses.
Decide whether the player is transferred, copied or remotely controlling bodies, and what
maintains continuity. Technology enabling this should have observable limits before the crisis.

Act III uses known locations as remotely affected spaces. Access still requires reachable
nodes, hosts or allies; nonphysical existence does not grant universal access. Physical tasks
may require cooperation. The loss of a body changes verbs and relationships while preserving
the value of everything learned. Whether a physical anchor remains, and whether any body is
recovered in the ending, are open story decisions.

## 6. Learning and capability progression

Separate ambient exposure, guided introduction, independent practice and combination. Seeing
a crane at work is not learning to hack it. Receiving a module is not demonstrating mastery.
Introduce one principal new demand per teaching encounter; combine it with familiar actions.

| Concept | First formal teaching | Practice and later development |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Movement, interaction, items | Prologue harbor tasks | Commute, home, then independent Downtown exploration |
| Observation and ordinary information | Read a task or notice with an immediate use | Act I clues become tools; later evidence spans places |
| Direct hacking and device state | Early Act I, one device and one useful verb | Repeat on another device; inspect visible consequences |
| Events and wiring | Act I, one visible source and target | Independent wire, then cutting/replacing a pre-existing wire |
| Information and access | Act I, nearby clue and related simple guard | Known connections before scanning; later networks and sectors |
| Logic | After reliable independent wiring; provisionally Act II | Teach TIMER and SWITCH separately, then combine |
| Time and environment | Ambient in prologue; persistent state in Act I | Act II teaches recurring rhythms, schedules and environmental constraints separately |
| Failure and danger | Harmless, recoverable mistakes from the start | Explicit security pressure in Act II after safe rehearsal |
| Resources | Ordinary wages/purchases in prologue | Optional exchange in Act I; production and tradeoffs in Act II |
| Remote action | Limited preparation before Act III | Distributed control and cooperation in Act III |

Basic failure belongs early: a wrong action gives understandable feedback and can be undone.
Trace, watchdogs, narrow time windows and resource depletion are later pressures. Essential
learning and recovery must not depend on buying a hint with scarce currency.

### Nouns, verbs and interfaces

| Family | Proposed introduction |
| --- | --- |
| ACT | First hacking action: a familiar object, concrete verb and visible result |
| STORE | Read information already understood as a message, record or instruction |
| SENSE and events | First wire: show the triggering event and resulting action together |
| DAEMON | Encounter as a character first; expose formal machine interaction when useful |
| GUARD | A simple nearby clue and access relationship, without punitive first attempts |
| LINK | One known connection before discovery, scanning and network navigation |
| TIMER | A problem whose visible sequence creates a clear need for a delay |
| SWITCH | A separate need to enable/disable signal passage after wiring is familiar |
| CLOCK | Recurring device rhythm, distinct from the city day/night cycle |
| SEAL | Simple access can come earlier; multi-event sequence seals come after logic practice |
| LOG and WATCH | Introduce readable security feedback before active pursuit and repair |

TIMER and SWITCH are logic tools implemented using ACT nouns, not additional engine noun
kinds. Teach their behaviour without requiring the player to memorise internal taxonomy.
Exact verb lists should be chosen per encounter: do not expose a whole device vocabulary
when the lesson depends on one action.

Proposed interface sequence: everyday prompts → single-device hacking → event/verb wiring →
multi-device board → known connections → scan → net map → logic tools → advanced terminal
and remote workflows. Individual steps can move after scene design. The city map initially
serves the current route/district; its full layers need not appear together. Augmentation
should enable a first capability, not every hacking interface at once.

By the end of the prologue, players should independently move, interact, use a carried item,
recognise a goal and observe a consequence. By the end of Act I, they should independently
inspect a device, invoke a verb, explain and construct a simple wire, use a clue for access,
and recover from a harmless error. Timed multi-node security is not an Act I graduation requirement.

## 7. Scene access, maps and exposition

Treat **seen**, **known**, **currently accessible**, and **available for free return** as
different states. The harbor is visited during employment but need not be freely reachable
after deletion. A remembered district should stay on the map with an understandable access
restriction; do not erase geographical knowledge to enforce the act boundary.

During the prologue, work assignments and the commute establish a bounded route. During
Act I, transport and service permissions can explain confinement to Downtown. Restrictions
need to agree across walking exits, metro, taxi and quick travel. The present Galleria-to-market
service corridor must not accidentally bypass the intended act boundary.

Ambient priming can show silhouettes, robots, security and distant districts. Focused exposition
needs a clear object or person, an action that makes the information matter, and space afterward.
Introduce institutions through their effects before naming their factions. Bring back people
and objects from the shift and neighbourhood so later changes have emotional reference points.

Give each required scene one main player objective, one central story contribution and one
principal new learning demand at most. Optional activity should remain recognisably optional
and should not introduce an unexplained advanced interface during an early lesson.

## 9. Detailed design and remaining questions

Milestone 1 is now documented in [PROLOGUE-ACT-I-PLAN.md](./PROLOGUE-ACT-I-PLAN.md).
The [roadmap](../../ROADMAP.md) defines the build sequence. The plan supplies working defaults
for the prologue and Downtown learning spine; later embodiment decisions remain open.

The scene-by-scene matrix uses:

`story beat → place → objective → new concept → prior practice → available verbs/interfaces →
access conditions → failure/recovery → observable understanding → persistent consequence`.

The plan addresses the first four questions with revisable defaults. Refine them during
chapter design; resolve the later story questions before implementing their transitions:

1. Which harbor tasks and recurring people make the shift satisfying in its own right?
2. What exact morning denial starts Act I, and how does the player reach Kessel?
3. Which Downtown encounters teach and rehearse each foundational hacking concept?
4. Which existing advanced puzzles move later, and what earns the Act I departure?
5. What causes the transfer, where is the human body, and what happens to Kessel and Moth?
6. What is transferred between bodies, what anchors remote presence, and what can be restored?
7. How do the Registry evidence, factions and possible endings serve this personal arc?

Later player testing should check understanding, attention and independent action, not just
completion. Pacing targets should follow those observations; no fixed tutorial duration is
approved here. This concept does not initiate a playthrough or implementation.
